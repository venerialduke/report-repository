One in five customers drives 59% of revenue: our six customer segments for 2026

A refreshed segmentation of 2.3M active customers, built on recency, frequency, spend, category mix and channel. It replaces the 2023 model and is now available as a field in the warehouse and in Braze.

By Daniel Reyes & Priya Natarajan Published Apr 22, 2026 Data through Mar 31, 2026

The short version

  1. Revenue is concentrated. Loyal Members and Nest Builders are 26% of active customers and 59% of trailing-12-month revenue. One-and-Done shoppers are a quarter of customers but 4% of revenue.
  2. Two segments are new since 2023. Big-Ticket Planners (6% of customers, 15% of revenue, AOV $601) and Deal Hunters (19% of customers, mostly buying on promotion) were hidden inside the old “Mid-value” segment.
  3. The model is stable enough to use. Bootstrapped assignments agree 87% of the time (adjusted Rand index 0.87), and the segments predict next-6-month purchase rates from 17% to 91%.
  4. How to use it: Retention and CRM should target by segment rather than one RFM score. Merchandising should plan big & bulky around Big-Ticket Planners. Media should stop paying to re-acquire One-and-Done buyers from marketplaces.

01Six segments, each with a different job for the business

We built the segments from how customers actually shop: how recently, how often, how much, what they buy and where they buy it. We then named them so that someone in merchandising or CRM can guess the behaviour from the name alone.

Pine+ membership was not an input to the clustering. We left it out on purpose so the segments describe behaviour, not a status we assign. Even so, 84% of Loyal Members turn out to be members, which suggests the programme reaches the right people. It also means about 52,000 non-member Loyal Members already shop like members and are the most obvious membership prospects we have.

02Loyal Members and Nest Builders are a quarter of customers but three-fifths of revenue

Customer counts and revenue look very different. Loyal Members are 14% of customers and 38% of revenue. One-and-Done shoppers are 25% of customers and 4% of revenue. Big-Ticket Planners have the widest gap: they are only 6% of customers, but each is worth nearly as much as a Loyal Member because of AOV rather than frequency.

Why this matters for 2026 plan

FP&A’s 2026 plan needs about $100M of growth ($1.14B to $1.24B). Moving 5% of Nest Builders into Loyal Member behaviour would add about $7M a year. Getting 5% of One-and-Done buyers to place a second order adds under $3M. Where we spend retention effort should reflect that difference.

03Category and channel mix separate segments that spend the same amount

With RFM alone, Big-Ticket Planners and Loyal Members look alike: both spend about $1,300 a year. What separates them is what they buy. Furniture makes up 71% of Big-Ticket spend and 22% of Loyal Member spend. Deal Hunters and Occasional Refreshers spend similar amounts too, but Deal Hunters buy across kitchen and décor on promotion, while Refreshers buy full-price seasonal décor.

Channel mix matters most for the smallest-value segment. Loyal Members place half their orders in the app. One-and-Done buyers place 31% of theirs on marketplaces (Amazon and others), where we never get an email address and so cannot build a relationship. Across all segments, the order mix is 50% web, 40% app and 10% marketplace, which matches the company-wide split.

04The segments predict what customers do next, not just what they did

A segmentation is only useful if it predicts future behaviour. We assigned segments using data up to September 30, 2025, then measured purchases over the following six months. The ranking held up well. Nine in ten Loyal Members bought again, compared with one in six One-and-Done buyers. Big-Ticket Planners have a low repeat rate (38%), but that is expected: nobody buys a sofa every six months. Their value comes from the size of each order and from referrals.

05How teams should use the segments

From today, every active customer has a segment_2026 value in dw.dim_customer_segment_2026. It refreshes on the first Monday of each month and is synced to Braze as a user attribute. The 2023 rfm_tier field will be kept until June 30 and then removed.

  1. Retention and CRM: build journeys per segment. Start with a “settling in” journey for Nest Builders in months 2–6 after their first furniture order, which is the moment they could become Loyal Members, and a membership pitch to the 52k non-member Loyal Members. Owner: Retention squad & CRM · First journeys live by: Jun 1, 2026
  2. Merchandising: plan big & bulky around Big-Ticket Planners. They research for weeks and mostly buy on the web. Give them fabric swatches, room planners and white-glove delivery promises on the product page rather than discount codes. Owner: Furniture merchandising · Proposal by: May 29, 2026
  3. Marketing: stop paying to re-acquire One-and-Done marketplace buyers. Exclude them from paid-social retargeting audiences, where they convert at 0.4% in 2025. Move that spend to look-alikes of Nest Builders. Owner: Performance marketing (Lena Kowalski to support measurement) · By: May 15, 2026
  4. Promotions: cap Deal Hunter-only offers. Deal Hunters are 19% of customers but already buy 62% of their units on promotion. Extra targeted codes mostly subsidise orders that would have happened anyway. Owner: Pricing (Samuel Ortiz) · Review in Q3 promo calendar
Segments are behaviour, not identity

About 18% of customers change segment between monthly refreshes. The movement is mostly between Occasional Refreshers, Deal Hunters and One-and-Done. Use the segment as a targeting input and not as a permanent label, and don’t report year-on-year segment sizes without first checking against the stability backtest.

Methodology

Population
2.30M customers with at least one net-positive order in the 12 months to Mar 31, 2026 (the canonical active customer). Excludes trade/B2B accounts, employees and unmatched marketplace orders (about 3% of marketplace revenue).
Features
12 standardised features: log recency, log frequency and log net spend (RFM); share of spend in four category groups; share of orders by web, app and marketplace; share of units bought on promotion; and share of spend in big & bulky items. Pine+ status and demographics were left out deliberately.
Method
K-means with k-means++ initialisation, 50 restarts. We evaluated k = 4–9 using silhouette score (0.31 at k = 6, against 0.33 at k = 4 and 0.27 at k = 8), the elbow in within-cluster variance, and a workshop with CRM, Merchandising and Membership to check the segments were useful in practice. Stability was measured with the adjusted Rand index across 100 bootstrap samples.
Limitations
K-means favours clusters of similar size and spherical shape, so segment boundaries are approximate. Customers near a boundary (about 9%) are flagged with segment_confidence = 'low'. The house-move signal comes from a third-party address-change feed covering 71% of customers. Revenue is net of returns but before marketing costs, so segments are not ranked on profitability.
