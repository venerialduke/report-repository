“Complete the room” adds $0.32 per product-page session — about $9.6M a year

An 8-week holdout shows the carousel is truly incremental in furniture and bedding, and does almost nothing in decor. The next test should be the cart page.

By Priya Natarajan Published Mar 12, 2026 Holdout Jan 5 – Mar 1, 2026

The short version

  1. The carousel pays for itself. Product-page (PDP) sessions that saw “Complete the room” generated $6.44 of revenue, against $6.12 in the 10% holdout. That is +$0.32 per session (95% CI +$0.20 to +$0.44), or about $9.6M a year at current traffic.
  2. Last-click attribution overstates it by 75%. The dashboard credits the carousel with $0.56 per session. 43% of that revenue would have happened anyway, because customers find the same items through search or navigation.
  3. Impact varies a lot by category. Sofas & seating (+$0.52) and bedding (+$0.41) carry the result. In decor & storage, 90% of attributed revenue is cannibalised.
  4. Recommendation: test the carousel on the cart page in April, trim it to 4 tiles on mobile, and stop showing it on decor PDPs. Keep a permanent 5% holdout.

01The carousel adds 5% to product-page revenue, every week of the holdout

For the first time we have a clean counterfactual for the recommendation carousel. The answer is a real, steady effect that is smaller than our dashboards have claimed.

The Recommendations squad launched “Complete the room” on all web and app PDPs in September 2025. It shows up to eight products the model predicts go with the item being viewed. Until now its value was measured by last-click revenue, which counts every order that included a clicked carousel item. To measure what the carousel actually adds, we held out 10% of customers (randomised by customer ID) for eight weeks from January 5. Those customers saw the page with no carousel.

The weekly gap stayed between $0.29 and $0.35, so the effect is not a novelty spike. On average, exposed sessions produced $6.44 against $6.12 in the holdout, a +5.2% lift. The effect comes mainly from basket breadth rather than price. The share of orders containing products from two or more categories rose from 35.1% to 38.2%. AOV rose 4.4%, and conversion was almost unchanged (+0.8%, CI −1.9% to +3.5%). In other words, the carousel does not persuade more people to buy. It persuades buyers to add the matching rug or lamp.

02Almost half of the revenue we credit to the carousel would have happened anyway

The carousel dashboard reports $0.56 of revenue per PDP session. The holdout shows the true figure is $0.32.

The difference is cannibalisation. Many customers who click a carousel item would have reached the same product another way: they would have searched for “oak side table”, opened the collection page, or come back later from an email. Last-click attribution credits all of that revenue to the carousel. The holdout credits only what disappears when the carousel is removed. This matters for how Merchandising sells carousel placements to vendors, and for how the squad reports its OKRs.

So what

We should stop reporting last-click carousel revenue as “impact”. The squad's Q2 OKR should be set on incremental revenue per session, measured against the permanent holdout we recommend below.

03Furniture and bedding carry the effect; decor is almost pure cannibalisation

When the carousel completes a genuinely new need (a sofa next to a rug, a duvet next to sheets), it adds revenue. When it suggests small decor items customers would have found anyway, it adds almost nothing.

Decor & storage PDPs show the carousel to 17% of sessions but produce under 3% of its incremental revenue. On these pages the model mostly suggests more items from the same collection: the matching basket, the second vase. Those items are already linked in the “From this collection” strip just above. We see a smaller version of the same pattern in rugs, where the carousel often suggests rug pads that are also offered as an add-on in the buy box.

04Customers rarely look past the fourth tile

4.8% of PDP sessions click at least one carousel tile, and more than four in five of those clicks land on the first four slots.

On mobile, where the carousel adds about 180 px of scroll depth, dropping to four tiles would bring the reviews block up the page. That block is the second most-used element on PDPs. We would test this change rather than assume it, but the downside looks small: slots 5–8 account for only 0.8 pp of the 4.8% click-through, and on average they are the most cannibalised.

Guardrails

The carousel adds a median 40 ms to PDP render time. We saw no difference in PDP bounce rate (41.2% vs. 41.0%) or add-to-cart rate on the main item (8.9% vs. 8.9%). Carousel-attached items carry a 44% gross margin, slightly above the site average of 41%, so the revenue lift also holds on a margin basis.

05The cart page is the next place to test

The carousel works when it catches customers who have already decided on the main item and are open to finishing the room. The cart page is where most of those customers are.

Cart sessions have about four times the purchase intent of PDP sessions. Today the cart page shows only a generic “You may also like” strip, which has never been tested. If the cart carousel produced just half the per-session incremental revenue of the PDP version (+$0.16) across roughly 11M cart-page sessions a year, it would add $1.8M. At parity with the PDP it would add $3.5M. We recommend a 50/50 test starting in April and reading after four weeks, pre-registered with incremental revenue per cart session as the primary metric.

06Recommendations

  1. Run a 50/50 test of “Complete the room” on the cart page. Primary metric: incremental revenue per cart session; guardrail: checkout start rate. Owner: Recommendations squad with Priya Natarajan · Launch by: Apr 13, 2026
  2. Suppress the carousel on decor & storage PDPs. Replace it with “Complete the room” anchored on the room's furniture (e.g. show a sideboard next to a vase), and test the replacement. Owner: Recommendations squad · By: May 1, 2026
  3. Test a four-tile carousel on mobile. Owner: App squad · By: May 15, 2026
  4. Keep a permanent 5% customer holdout, and move the squad's OKR to incremental revenue per session. Owner: Head of Digital Product; Jonah Feld (metric build) · By: Q2 OKR setting
  5. Stop quoting last-click carousel revenue in vendor decks. Use the incremental figure instead. Owner: Merchandising · Effective immediately

Methodology

Design
Customer-level holdout (REC-HOLDOUT-01): 10% of logged-in and device-identified customers saw no carousel on any PDP. Randomised by hashing customer ID; sample-ratio check passed (p = 0.61).
Population
US web and app PDP sessions, Jan 5 – Mar 1, 2026: 4.6M sessions from 1.38M customers. Marketplace listings and out-of-stock PDPs (where the carousel is already suppressed) were excluded.
Metrics
Revenue per PDP session attributes all revenue from orders placed within the same session or within 24 hours of it, net of cancellations and 30-day returns. Customer-clustered standard errors; revenue winsorised at the 99.9th percentile. Category results are cut by the category of the viewed PDP and are not adjusted for multiplicity.
Annualisation
4.6M sessions over 8 weeks ≈ 29.9M PDP sessions a year × $0.32 ≈ $9.6M. At the permanent 5% holdout, realised value is about $9.1M.
Limitations
Off-peak period (January–February skews toward sale and bedding traffic). The 24-hour window may miss slower cross-session effects, such as a customer who comes back a week later for the rug. The cart-page estimate is an assumption, not a measurement.
