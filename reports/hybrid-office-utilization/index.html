Our offices peak on Tuesday–Thursday and sit mostly empty on Mondays and Fridays, so Denver could give back a floor

Ten weeks of badge and desk-booking data show Denver HQ never used more than 612 of its 900 desks. People rate office days as worth the commute when their own team is in, which is a matter of coordination, not of desk count.

By Hannah Liu Published Mar 30, 2026 Data Jan 5 – Mar 13, 2026 (10 weeks)
“The days I come in and my team isn't there, I spend the day on video calls from a desk I commuted 40 minutes to.”Denver HQ employee, February workplace pulse (paraphrased)

The short version

  1. Attendance is concentrated midweek. Denver desk utilisation averages 42% but runs from 11% on Fridays to 62% on Wednesdays. Chicago follows the same pattern at a lower level (31% average, 48% on Wednesdays).
  2. Denver's busiest day in 10 weeks used 612 desks (68%). That is 85% of the capacity of four floors. Denver could exercise the floor 6 give-back option (deadline Jun 30) and save about $1.1M a year, as long as midweek demand is spread more evenly.
  3. Teams with agreed anchor days are together 2.3× as often. On 63% of their office days at least half the team is in, compared with 27% for teams without anchor days. Employees whose team was in rated the day as “worth the commute” at 78%, compared with 36% when it wasn't.
  4. Recommendation: make team anchor days standard, assign them so Thursday takes some of Wednesday's load, and approve the floor 6 give-back.

01Both offices peak on Tuesday–Thursday and are close to empty on Fridays

We have no shortage of desks. The problem is timing: most people choose the same three days.

Across 48 working days, an average of 374 of Denver's 780 office-assigned employees badged in each day. That average hides a steep weekly pattern. Wednesday is busiest (62% of desks used), and Tuesday (57%) and Thursday (52%) are not far behind. Monday is at 26% and Friday at 11%. Chicago's 175 employees follow the same pattern at lower levels, peaking at 48% of its 240 desks on Wednesdays.

Individual attendance varies a lot. Denver employees averaged 2.4 office days a week and Chicago employees 2.1. Only 15% of Denver staff and 10% of Chicago staff come in four or more days, while 8–11% come in less than once a week. These low-attenders are mostly people whose managers and teams sit in the other office or work remotely.

02What makes an office day worthwhile is having your team there, not having a desk

Survey responses linked to badge data show that the value of a day in the office depends almost entirely on whether your team is there too.

The February workplace pulse (n = 612, 64% response) asked people to rate their most recent office day. With consent, we linked those ratings to badge data to see how much of the person's team was in that day.1 When fewer than a quarter of their team was in, 36% said the day was “worth the commute”. When three-quarters or more were in, 78% did.

The rest of the pulse tells the same story. 58% named “seeing my team” as their main reason to come in, well ahead of in-person meetings (41%) and a better setup than at home (22%).2 71% agreed that office days are better for collaboration, and 78% said focused work goes better at home. People are not avoiding the office. They are avoiding days when it can't give them what they come in for.

03Anchor days work, but only a third of teams have them

Teams that agree anchor days are together more than twice as often as teams that leave attendance to each individual.

We measured co-presence: the share of a person's office days on which at least half their team was also in. Teams with one or two agreed anchor days reached 63% on average. Teams without them reached 27%. Their overall attendance was about the same (2.4 vs. 2.3 days a week), so the difference comes from coordination, not from coming in more.

There is a catch. Four of the five teams with anchor days chose Tuesday or Wednesday. If every team copied them, Wednesday would move from 62% towards 80% and we would lose the headroom that makes the floor saving possible. Anchor days need to be assigned with the whole building in mind, not only chosen by each team.

Thursday is underused

Thursday is 10 points below Wednesday in Denver and 16 points below in Chicago. Moving two large teams without anchor days (Customer Apps and Supply Chain & Ops HQ, 222 people together) to a Tue + Thu anchor would raise co-presence for both. We estimate it would also lower Denver's busiest day by about 40 desks.

04Denver fits on four floors. Chicago should be resized when its lease renews in 2027

Even Denver's busiest days fit within four floors with room to spare. The fifth floor costs about $1.1M a year.

Denver HQ has five floors (2–6) of 180 desks each, or 900 desks. In 10 weeks the busiest day used 612 desks, and the typical weekly peak was 588. Four floors give 720 desks, so our worst day would have filled 85% of them. That is the level Workplace Services treats as the comfortable limit for booked desks, given that some desks are always out of use.

Floor 6 (about 21,000 rentable sq ft) has a give-back option in the lease. Written notice is due by June 30, 2026, and the floor would be surrendered on January 1, 2027. At a fully loaded cost of about $52/sq ft, that saves about $1.09M a year, less about $180k of one-off move and reconfiguration costs (FP&A estimate). The main risk is growth. At today's attendance, Denver corporate headcount would need to grow about 22% before a typical peak day filled all 720 desks. The 2026 headcount plan is +4%, which would take the typical peak to about 612 desks (85%).

Chicago's pattern supports resizing, but not yet. Its 95th-percentile day used 131 desks, more than one floor of 120. The lease renews in August 2027. We recommend going into that renewal aiming for about 150 desks rather than 240.

What could change the answer

January to March is a seasonally average quarter for office attendance. Our 2025 data shows September–October running about 5% higher. Allowing for that, the typical weekly peak would reach about 620 desks (86% of four floors) in the busiest months. That is still workable, but only with the Thursday rebalancing described above.

Methodology

Population
955 employees assigned to an office in Workday (Denver 780, Chicago 175). Remote employees and visitors are excluded from per-employee measures but included in the desk counts (about 3% of daily badge-ins).
Period
Jan 5 – Mar 13, 2026: 10 weeks, 48 working days after excluding two company holidays.
Utilisation
Unique people per day with a badge-in, divided by the number of assignable desks. A cross-check against desk check-ins agreed within 2 points, since most people who badge in also check in to a desk.
Co-presence
For each person-day in the office, whether at least 50% of their Workday team (same manager, teams of 10 or more) also badged in that day. Averaged per person, then per team.
Survey linkage
612 pulse respondents; 488 consented to linkage. Results are shown only for groups of 10 or more. People Analytics performed the linkage, and badge data was never shown to managers at individual level.
Limitations
Badge data misses tailgating (estimated 2–4% of entries). We have one quarter of data and seasonality is estimated from 2025. People who consented to linkage may differ from those who did not: their overall attendance was 0.2 days a week higher.

1 Linkage used a salted employee key held by People Analytics. Only aggregates were exported for this report.

2 Respondents could choose up to two reasons, so the percentages add up to more than 100%.
